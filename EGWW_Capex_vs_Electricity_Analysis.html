EGWW capital expenditure vs electricity prices

Quarterly, 1987Q3 – 2025Q4. Sources: ABS 5625.0 Table 8 (Trend, Chain Volume Measures, EGWW sector) and ABS 6401.0 Table 18 (Electricity, weighted average of eight capital cities).

Key takeaways

Headline finding

In levels, EGWW capex and the electricity CPI move strongly together (Pearson r = +0.90, R² = 0.81). In year-on-year percentage changes, that relationship vanishes (r = -0.12, not statistically significant). The levels relationship is almost entirely a co-trend artefact — both series rose secularly through four decades — rather than evidence of a tight cyclical link between investment and prices.

Lag analysis is consistent with this: in levels every lag from -8 to +8 quarters shows ~r = 0.90 (i.e. no informative lead/lag structure beyond shared trend). In YoY changes, the strongest correlation appears at lag +8 (electricity leading by 8 quarters) at r = -0.29 — suggesting price surges weakly precede capex slowdowns, but with high noise.

6 · Interpretation

Why the levels and YoY% results disagree

Both EGWW capex and the electricity index display strong unit-root behaviour with clear breaks: capex steps up after privatisation and the start of large network-augmentation programs in the late 1990s and again during the 2010s renewables build; the electricity index steps up after wholesale-market crises in the late 2000s (drought/GFC), 2017 (gas-driven East Coast crunch), and 2022 (Russian invasion). The level-on-level correlation captures the fact that both series have risen — but it gives no information about cyclical co-movement.

Differencing the series (here as 4-quarter percentage changes) removes the trend and exposes the cyclical relationship. That relationship is weak and indistinguishable from zero, with a slight negative tilt that does not reach statistical significance.

What this implies about the underlying mechanism

  • Capex is not strongly responsive to short-term price signals. Quarterly fluctuations in retail electricity prices do not predict quarterly fluctuations in EGWW investment. This is consistent with regulated-asset-base investment cycles — Western Power, Powerlink, AusNet etc. invest according to multi-year regulatory determinations, not retail price signals.
  • Causality, where it exists, likely runs from capex to prices, not the reverse. Under the AER post-tax revenue model (and its WA equivalent), network capex feeds directly into the regulated asset base, which is recovered from retail customers over the asset life. This is the standard explanation for the "gold plating" period (2008–2014) that produced the most visible run-up in the price series. The lag +8 negative correlation in YoY% is consistent with prices rising in response to past investment, then dampening subsequent capex through demand response or political pressure for tariff relief.
  • Sector capex is dominated by network infrastructure, not generation. EGWW in chain-volume terms grew ~50× from 1987 to 2025; total equipment capex grew at a much slower pace. The capex run-up is therefore primarily about poles-and-wires and supporting infrastructure rather than generation plant — which fits the narrative of declining coal generation, intermittent renewable build, and accelerating transmission spend for the energy transition.
  • The high contemporaneous level correlation is not evidence of price-driven investment. A common false reading of these numbers would be "high prices drove high investment" or vice versa. The data does not support that. What the data does support is a long-run accumulation story: progressive investment in the sector through structural reform, demand growth, and now decarbonisation, with electricity prices in retail-CPI terms rising over the same period for largely overlapping reasons (cost-of-service regulation, market-design changes, fuel shocks).

Caveats

  • The capex series is the trend chain-volume measure, which smooths short-run noise but also dampens cyclical co-movement.
  • EGWW includes Gas, Water and Waste Services alongside Electricity. The Electricity-only component is not separately published in this table.
  • The CPI electricity series reflects retail prices to households, not wholesale prices or commercial/industrial tariffs. The wholesale price linkage to capex decisions would be tighter.
  • The post-2022 retail-price values are distorted by Commonwealth and state bill-relief rebates which the ABS treats as price reductions, materially affecting recent observations.
