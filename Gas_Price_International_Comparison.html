Methodology note

Australian series: Two series shown. National (blue) — ABS 6401.0 Table 18, Gas & other household fuels CPI, weighted average of eight capital cities (Series A2331921F, Sep 2025 = 100). Perth/WA (teal dashed) — ABS 6401.0 Table 9 legacy quarterly (Series A2331901W, Sep 1989–Jun 2019) chain-linked to Table 10 monthly (Series A130392607J, Oct 2021–Jan 2026), averaged to quarters and indexed to Sep 1989 = 100. The Sep 2019–Sep 2021 gap is bridged using national gas CPI proportional changes. Both series are retail CPIs including wholesale gas, network charges, retailer margins and applicable levies.

International series: World Bank Pink Sheets monthly wholesale spot prices in USD/MMBtu — US Henry Hub, European gas (NBP/TTF composite), Japan LNG (CIF). Monthly values averaged to ABS-aligned quarters (Jul–Sep = Sep quarter, Oct–Dec = Dec quarter, Jan–Mar = Mar quarter, Apr–Jun = Jun quarter).

Comparison basis: All series rebased to 100 at Sep 1989. This shows relative growth, not absolute price levels — comparing retail AUS CPI to wholesale USD/MMBtu in levels is not meaningful. Growth rates and turning points are the informative signal.

Analysis: WA Domestic Gas Reservation Policy

Key question

Pre-reservation baseline: NWS–SECWA take-or-pay contracts (1985–2005)

Before the 2006 Reservation Policy existed, WA domestic gas prices were anchored by long-term take-or-pay contracts between the North West Shelf (NWS) Joint Venture and the State Energy Commission of Western Australia (SECWA). Contracts were signed in September 1980, ran from 1985 to 2005, and committed 370 million cubic feet per day (MMCFD) of gas — with SECWA obligated to draw 351 MMCFD (95%). Alcoa was SECWA's largest on-sold customer, absorbing approximately 150 MMCFD, over half of the gas piped to the South West of the state.

The contracted upstream price was never publicly disclosed (strict confidentiality clauses applied), but was indexed to competing fuels using coal- and oil-competitive pricing formulas. Contemporary sources indicate WA upstream prices were held at approximately AUD $2–3 per gigajoule throughout this period.

Unit note — MMCFD vs gigajoules: MMCFD (million cubic feet per day) is a volumetric flow rate; gigajoules (GJ) measure energy content. The conversion depends on the calorific value of the gas: at the standard natural gas heating value of approximately 1,055 BTU per standard cubic foot, 1 MMCFD ≈ 1,055 MMBtu/day ≈ 1,113 GJ/day. The 370 MMCFD NWS–SECWA contracted volume therefore represents roughly 411 TJ/day, or approximately 150 PJ/year of energy delivery. The AUD $2–3/GJ price converts to AUD $2.11–3.16/MMBtu (÷ 0.948, since 1 GJ = 0.9479 MMBtu). At the AUD/USD exchange rates of that era (~0.70–0.85), this equates to roughly USD $1.50–2.70/MMBtu — broadly in line with contemporaneous US Henry Hub prices and well below Japan LNG CIF (which ranged USD $3.3–5.5/MMBtu over 1989–2005).

The expiry of these contracts around 2005 removed the regulated price floor that had kept WA upstream gas anchored below market-clearing levels. The annotation on the chart marks this transition. The 2006 Domestic Gas Reservation Policy was introduced in direct response to the supply-security concern created by the contract void — ensuring that new LNG export projects could not absorb the entire incremental gas supply and push domestic prices to export parity.

Background

The WA Domestic Gas Reservation Policy was announced September 2006 and took full legal effect from 2011. It requires each new LNG export project to set aside 15% of its gas reserves for the WA domestic market, preventing full export-parity pricing of the kind that affected eastern Australia after 2014. The policy was a direct response to concerns that LNG export growth — particularly from the North West Shelf — would draw WA's domestic gas supply offshore and raise retail and industrial prices toward Asian LNG spot levels.

What the indexed chart shows

Three phases are visible in Australian gas CPI relative to international benchmarks:

  • 1989–2005 (NWS–SECWA contract era): WA upstream gas was anchored at ~AUD $2–3/GJ under the 20-year NWS take-or-pay contracts. Retail gas CPI grew slowly (~2–3% p.a.), broadly tracking general CPI inflation. International wholesale prices were volatile but mean-reverting. The contract expiry in 2005 (annotated on chart) marked the end of this regulated-price era.
  • 2000–2006 (early pressure): US and European prices spiked (2000–01 energy crisis, 2005 Hurricane Katrina). Australian gas CPI continued its steady climb. The WA Gas Reservation Policy was announced at the end of this period.
  • 2006–2025 (post-policy, diverging pressures): National Australian gas CPI accelerated significantly from 2012 onward — driven by the east coast LNG export buildout (QCLNG, APLNG, GLNG — operational 2014–16), which pushed eastern-state domestic prices toward export parity. The Perth series (teal dashed) visibly diverges from the national blue line from ~2014, growing at a materially slower rate. The carbon tax (Jul 2012–Jul 2014) added a further cost layer visible as a step-jump in Sep 2012, affecting both series equally as a federal charge.
Key finding — Perth gas grew materially slower than the national average: From Sep 2006 (policy announcement) to Dec 2025, Perth gas CPI rose approximately +161% while the national weighted average rose +203%. The divergence is visible in the chart from ~2012 onward — the point at which east coast LNG export terminals came online and pushed eastern-state domestic prices toward export parity. Western Australia, protected by the 15% reservation requirement, did not experience the same supply squeeze. That said, the divergence between Perth and the national average is not solely attributable to the reservation policy. In earlier years, WA gas competed with coal for electricity generation, which capped the price gas could command in the domestic market. In more recent years, the growing role of renewable energy and batteries has introduced a further competitive constraint on gas pricing in WA — independent of reservation policy settings. The reservation policy is one factor among several that have kept Perth gas prices below the east coast trajectory.

Japan LNG — the most relevant comparator for WA

Japan is Australia's largest LNG customer, and the Japan LNG price (CIF) represents the approximate export opportunity cost for WA gas. If the reservation policy is working, WA domestic prices should rise less than Japan LNG over the same horizon. From Sep 2006 to Dec 2025, Japan LNG rose +54% while national Australian gas CPI rose +203%. The Japan LNG path — spike 2011–14 post-Fukushima, then decline as global LNG supply expanded — confirms that WA's reservation policy set a ceiling well below the Asian LNG market clearing price during the tightest period.

The US Henry Hub counter-case

The shale gas revolution (visible in the indexed chart from 2009) collapsed US Henry Hub prices. By Dec 2025 the US index sits below 240 — less than half the Australian CPI index — illustrating what fully insulating a domestic market from LNG export arbitrage can achieve. WA's reservation policy is philosophically similar, if less dramatic in effect.

Key structural events (annotated on chart)

  • Sep 2000 — GST introduction: Step-up in Australian gas CPI as the Goods and Services Tax was applied to domestic energy.
  • Sep 2005 — NWS–SECWA contracts expire: The 20-year take-or-pay contracts that anchored WA upstream gas at ~AUD $2–3/GJ ended, removing the regulated price floor and creating the supply-security concern that motivated the 2006 Reservation Policy.
  • Sep 2006 — WA Gas Reservation Policy: Announced. Key policy inflection for WA; east coast unaffected.
  • Sep 2012 — Carbon Tax: Sharpest single-quarter jump in Australian gas CPI (+14.2%). Federal carbon price added directly to retail gas costs.
  • Jul 2014 — Carbon Tax repeal: Gas CPI stabilised but did not fall — rising east coast supply costs offset the removal of the carbon charge.
  • Mar 2016 — East coast LNG exports at capacity: Three major Queensland LNG projects fully operational. Eastern domestic gas supply tightened toward export parity. WA insulated.
  • Mar 2020 — COVID-19: Demand collapse briefly suppressed international gas prices; Australian CPI sticky.
  • Mar 2022 — Russia–Ukraine war: European gas prices spiked to historic highs. Japan LNG rose moderately. Australian gas CPI rose 10–15% over 2022–23 via global LNG market tightening and east coast pricing pass-through.
Caveat — chain-link gap and series methodology: The Perth series splices two ABS methodologies (legacy Table 9 quarterly, ending Jun 2019; current Table 10 monthly, starting Oct 2021). The Sep 2019–Sep 2021 gap is estimated by applying national gas CPI proportional changes to Perth's Jun 2019 level — this is a reasonable approximation but not observed Perth data. Index levels before and after the splice should be interpreted as indicative of trend direction rather than precise point-in-time values. For a fully precise city-level comparison (Perth vs Sydney vs Melbourne), use the raw ABS 6401010 Energy Summary sheet directly.

Tentative verdict

The Perth-specific series now visible in the chart strengthens the policy case considerably. Perth gas CPI diverged from the national average from approximately 2012–14 onward — exactly when east coast LNG export terminals came online and eastern domestic prices began tracking Asian LNG spot levels. Perth's post-policy growth of ~161% versus the national ~203% represents a ~42 percentage point containment effect over 2006–2025. Japan LNG (the relevant opportunity-cost benchmark for WA gas exporters) rose only ~54% over the same period, confirming that WA's reservation floor sat well below Asian market clearing prices and that the policy successfully broke the link between export parity and domestic retail prices.
